Method. Same as Cooley, Hubbard & Walz (1998). Start with a portfolio of 1, withdraw the chosen rate in year one, then either the same nominal amount every year or an amount that grows with that market's CPI. Between withdrawals the portfolio earns the stock/bond mix's actual return for that calendar year and is rebalanced. A window succeeds if the balance is still positive after the last year. Do this for every start year the data allows, and the success rate is the share of windows that survived. The safe rate is the highest rate, on a 0.05% grid, whose success rate meets your requirement. Fees are deducted from the whole portfolio every year (default 0.8%; the original study had none).

Spending flexibility (optional). With the cut enabled, any year in which the portfolio stands below the chosen share of its starting value, measured in real terms under the inflation-adjusted rule (nominal under the fixed rule), the withdrawal is reduced by the chosen percentage. Spending returns to the full amount as soon as the portfolio is back above the line. The early-years rule is a second line, usually higher, that only applies during the first Y years, when a bad sequence of returns does the most damage; if both rules trigger in the same year the larger cut is used, not both. The safe rate is then the full, uncut rate you start from; the card shows how many windows had to cut at 4% and for how many years on average. Neither rule is part of the original Trinity study.

Outcomes by age. The stacked chart replays every historical window for one market at the chosen withdrawal rate and, for each year of the horizon, sorts the windows by the portfolio's real value relative to its start: broke, below the start, 1–2×, 2–5× and above 5×. With the mortality wedge on, each band is scaled by the probability of still being alive at that age (SSA period life table, by sex) and the grey band is the cumulative probability of having died. "Run out while still alive" sums, over the years, the share of windows that go broke that year times the chance of being alive to see it.

Data. US: Shiller's S&P Composite and 10-year Treasury total returns and US CPI, 1871→. Japan, UK, Germany: Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database, local equity and long government bond total returns and local CPI, 1870–2020, extended to 2025. World: GDP-weighted USD equity return of the 18 JST countries to 1969, then MSCI World in USD, with US Treasuries and US CPI (a USD investor in a global fund).

Caveats. Long windows overlap heavily, so a "95% success" over 60-year windows rests on only two or three independent histories. Japanese equities are missing for 1946–47 and German bonds for 1944–48; windows that need those years are skipped, not scored, which removes most pre-war Japanese windows and all German windows with bonds that cross the war. Germany's 1923 hyperinflation is in the data and makes the fixed-nominal rule meaningless there. No taxes.
