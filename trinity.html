Trinity Study, Longer Horizons

The 1998 Trinity study asked how often a stock/bond portfolio survived 15–30 years of withdrawals over 1926–1995 in the US. This page re-runs the same experiment on Shiller's S&P Composite series from 1871 to 2025, extends the payout periods to 35, 40, 45, 50 and 60 years, and repeats it for Japan, the UK, Germany and a global-equity proxy. Every cell is computed live in your browser.

How to read this. Each cell is the share of overlapping historical start years in which the portfolio still had money at the end of the payout period. at or above threshold; 75% up to threshold; 50–75%; below 50%. Hover a cell to see which start years failed.

Mechanics. Annual steps, rebalanced to the target mix each year. The first withdrawal is the rate × starting balance; under the inflation-adjusted rule it then grows with US CPI, under the nominal rule it stays fixed. A period succeeds if the balance is still positive at the end. For the US this mirrors Cooley, Hubbard & Walz (1998) except that bonds are 10-year Treasuries (Shiller's constant-maturity total-return series) rather than long-term high-grade corporates, and the stock series is Shiller's S&P Composite (monthly-average prices, January to January) rather than Ibbotson's month-end S&P 500. Japan, the UK and Germany use the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database (equity and long-term government bond total returns, local CPI, all in local currency) through 2020, extended to 2025 with MSCI / local index data. The world proxy is a GDP-weighted average of the 18 JST countries' USD equity returns up to 1969, then MSCI World in USD, paired with US Treasuries and US CPI (a USD investor holding a global fund).

Spending flexibility (optional). With the cut enabled, any year in which the portfolio is below the chosen share of its starting value (real terms under the inflation-adjusted rule, nominal under the fixed rule) the withdrawal is reduced by the chosen percentage, and restored once the portfolio recovers. The early-years rule is a second, usually higher line that applies only in the first Y years; when both trigger, the larger cut is used. Neither is part of the original study.

Gaps and wars. Japanese equity returns are missing for 1946–47 and German bond returns for 1944–48 (markets closed, then currency reform). A window that needs a missing value is left out of the count rather than scored, so wartime start years are under-represented for those two markets. Germany's 1923 hyperinflation is in the data: under the fixed-nominal rule any withdrawal becomes worthless and "succeeds", so read Germany with the inflation-adjusted rule only.

Longer periods, fewer independent samples. 60-year windows starting 1871–1966 overlap heavily: there are really only two or three non-overlapping 60-year histories in the data. Treat the long-horizon rows as "what has happened", not as a probability. The 1871–1925 stretch also predates the S&P 500 itself; Shiller splices Cowles Commission data for those years.

Data. data/markets.js, generated by scripts/build_data.py and scripts/build_markets.py. The same tables can be produced offline with scripts/trinity.py --market …; see the README.
