Notes on the model

Cycles start in 1971 (MSCI World inception) through 2024. For a 40-year horizon there are 16 fully overlapping cycles; for 30 years, 26; for 20 years, 36. Past frequency ≠ future probability; treat tail bands as suggestive, not predictive.

Mechanics. Each simulated year, returns are applied to the portfolio (MSCI World / PIMCO Income / US 10-yr / US T-bill weighted by allocation), then fees, then the SGD spending amount — inflated by Singapore CPI — is converted to USD at that year's USD/SGD rate and withdrawn. If the portfolio falls below the threshold (measured in real SGD purchasing power), spending switches to the cut-down annual budget — which also inflates with SG CPI — and switches back once the portfolio recovers above the threshold.

PIMCO Income. Actual PIMIX (institutional class) total returns for 2008–2025. For 1971–2007 (the fund did not exist) we proxy with US 10-yr Treasury returns; this is conservative, since PIMCO Income historically beats Treasuries on credit and structuring.

Mortality. SSA period life table. The grey band is the cumulative probability of being dead by that age, stacked on top of the financial outcomes.

USD/SGD. By default the rate moves with history. Check "Fix USD/SGD rate" to pin it at a constant value (default 1.25) — useful for isolating portfolio risk from currency risk, or for stress-testing a specific FX assumption. The worst-case panel below uses 1.20 by default, or your fixed rate if you've set it below 1.20.

Not modelled. Taxes, Singapore property, CPF, healthcare, gifting separately from spending (rolled into the annual spending number).

About this scenario. This is a single deterministic path — not a probability distribution. It answers a different question: "of all the actual 40-year (or N-year) periods we have data for, what would have happened if you'd retired at the start of the worst one, AND the SGD had been at its strongest historical point throughout?" Equity returns are the S&P 500 — not MSCI World — because Shiller's long-run series only covers US. Spending is inflated by US CPI from the same period since Singapore CPI doesn't exist that far back. PIMCO Income, which started in 2007, is proxied by US 10-year Treasury returns whenever the worst window predates the fund's inception.
International worst-case · Japan / UK / Germany · 1970→

If your equities had been somewhere else.

For each of Japan, UK, and Germany, find the worst N-year window for that country's equity market (using local-currency real total returns), then replay that exact sequence assuming your entire portfolio had been denominated in that currency. The SGD is pinned at 10% stronger than that currency's historical SGD-strongest level for the whole horizon.

About this scenario. Equity returns use MSCI Japan / FTSE All-Share / MSCI Germany in local currency. Spending inflates by local CPI (since the question is "what if my whole life had been in that economy"). The non-equity portion (PIMCO/bonds/cash combined) earns local government bond returns — PIMCO Income has no Japan/UK/Germany historical equivalent. Currency assumption: SGD is pinned at 10% stronger than its historical peak strength against each currency, applied throughout. Japan 1989+ is the canonical lost-decades sequence; UK 1973-74 included a 51% equity crash with 24% inflation; Germany's worst windows are mostly the dot-com / GFC combinations.
